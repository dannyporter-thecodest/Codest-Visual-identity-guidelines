# Should you hire in-house or extend your engineering team?

Hire in-house when the work is core to your product for years and you can wait for the right person. Extend your engineering team with an external one when a roadmap item, a missing skill or a test of a new direction cannot wait for hiring. Many teams do both: extend now, and hire for the long term.

**TL;DR**

- 57.5% of EU enterprises that tried to recruit ICT specialists in 2023 found the vacancies hard to fill.
- Hire in-house for long-term product knowledge, a clear role and enough time to choose well.
- Extend your team when the work is urgent, needs a skill you lack, or is still uncertain.
- The real risk of extending is losing control, so agree the people, the access and the exit first.
- Handing over a whole defined area is a third option when you want one owner for an outcome.

## Why is hiring engineers in-house so slow in Europe?

Hiring is slow because engineering roles are hard to fill: in 2023, 57.5% of EU enterprises that recruited or tried to recruit ICT specialists had difficulties filling the vacancies, according to [Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=ICT_specialists_-_statistics_on_hard-to-fill_vacancies_in_enterprises).

The share is higher in some of our core markets. Eurostat reports 72.41% for Germany and 67.26% for Austria in the same year.

A hard-to-fill vacancy rarely stays a hiring problem. It becomes a roadmap problem: the feature waits, other engineers cover the gap, and the team loses focus on what matters most.

None of this means you should stop hiring. It means the hiring plan and the roadmap plan need to be decided separately.

**What this means for you:** Plan the roadmap on the people you have today, and treat every open role as a date you cannot rely on.

## When should you extend your engineering team instead of hiring?

Extend your engineering team when the need is urgent, specific or uncertain: a roadmap item that cannot slip, a skill your team does not have, or a direction you want to test before committing to permanent roles.

Team extension means adding external engineers who work inside your team, your process and your tools, while you keep control of the product. Our [Staff Augmentation model](https://thecodest.co/en/services/staff-augmentation/) works this way.

SupplierPlus, an invoice financing platform based in Tallinn, is one example. It needed a secure platform for digital documents, electronic signatures and automatic data exchange, and [brought in a team of Ruby developers](https://thecodest.co/en/case-studies/providing-a-team-of-ruby-developers-for-a-fintech-company/) from The Codest to build it.

Extension also works as a bridge. External engineers carry the roadmap while you hire, then hand over context to the people you bring in.

**What this means for you:** Write down why the need cannot wait. If you cannot name the deadline or the missing skill, hiring may be the better route.

## When is hiring in-house the better choice?

Hire in-house when the work is core to your product for years, when the knowledge must stay inside the company, and when the role is clear enough to hire for well.

- **Core, long-lived knowledge.** The people who own your architecture and product direction should usually be on your payroll.
- **A stable, well-defined role.** If you can describe the job for the next two years, you can hire for it.
- **Time to choose well.** A rushed permanent hire is harder to undo than an external engineer who is not the right fit.

Hiring is also how a team builds its own culture and standards. External engineers can follow and strengthen them, but they should not be the only people who hold them.

## How do hiring, team extension and handing over an area compare?

Hiring gives you the most control and the slowest start, team extension adds capacity inside your own process, and handing over a defined area gives one external owner responsibility for an agreed outcome.

*Three ways to add engineering capacity*

| Option | Best when | Main risk | Agree before you start |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hire in-house | The role is core, stable and worth waiting for | The roadmap waits while the vacancy stays open | Which work pauses until the hire starts |
| Extend your team | Work is urgent, needs a missing skill, or is still uncertain | Losing control of who works on what | Who joins, how work is reviewed, how you scale down |
| Hand over a defined area | You want one owner for a whole product area or system | Unclear scope and success criteria | Scope, success criteria, reporting and the exit plan |

The third option fits when you need an outcome rather than extra people. A major logistics company, for example, [built a custom logistics platform](https://thecodest.co/en/case-studies/transforming-logistics-with-a-custom-digital-platform/) with The Codest to automate manual order processing and route planning. That is the [Product Development](https://thecodest.co/en/services/product-development/) model.

**What this means for you:** Choose the model by the decision you need to make, not by the day rate. The cheapest option per month can be the most expensive per outcome.

## What are the risks of extending your team, and how do you reduce them?

The main risk of extending your team is losing control: people you did not choose, work you cannot see, and knowledge that leaves with the vendor. Agree the people, the access and the exit before anyone starts.

- **The team in the pitch is not the team that delivers.** The fix: meet and interview the engineers who will do the work, before you sign.
- **Work happens out of sight.** The fix: external engineers work in your repository, tools and rituals, not in a separate queue.
- **Knowledge leaves with the vendor.** The fix: agree documentation, code ownership and a handover period in the contract.
- **Nobody owns the outcome.** The fix: name one owner on each side for the agreed scope, and review it at a fixed rhythm.

## Key insights

- Engineering vacancies are hard to fill across the EU, so do not plan the roadmap on open roles.
- Hire in-house for core, long-term roles you can describe and wait for.
- Extend your team for urgent, specialist or uncertain work, and keep control of the product.
- Hand over a defined area when you want one owner for an outcome.
- Agree the people, access, ownership and exit before an external engineer starts.

## Related questions

### Is extending a team cheaper than hiring?

Not always per month. Compare the total cost: time to hire, recruitment fees, onboarding, and what it costs when a roadmap item waits. Ask any vendor how quickly you can scale the team down as well as up.

### Can external engineers work inside our own process?

They should. Good team extension means external engineers use your repository, tickets, reviews and meetings, follow your standards, and report to your engineering lead. If a vendor insists on its own separate process, you are buying a project, not an extension.
